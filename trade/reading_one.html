The gravity model
Gravity Model of Trade
Tij = A × (Yi × Yj) / Dij
Tij — Trade volume between countries Yi, Yj — GDP of each country Dij — Distance between them A — Constant factor

Larger economies trade more

Countries with higher GDP produce and consume more, naturally importing and exporting larger volumes of goods and services.

GDP drives trade

Distance reduces trade

Farther countries face higher shipping costs, longer transit times, and less familiarity — all of which reduce the volume of trade.

Proximity matters

Trade barriers matter

Borders, tariffs, and regulatory differences act like extra distance — they reduce trade even when countries are geographically close.

Evidence table 4 · Historical timeline
History of globalization and world trade
Pre-1914
First age of globalization
Rapid expansion of international trade powered by steamships, railways, and the gold standard. Trade-to-GDP ratios reached levels not seen again until the 1990s.
1914–1945
Collapse of trade
World Wars and the Great Depression devastated global trade. Protectionist policies like the US Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act further shrank cross-border commerce.
1945–1970s
Rebuilding and GATT
Post-WWII institutions (GATT, IMF, World Bank) lowered barriers. Trade recovered steadily as Europe rebuilt and Japan industrialized.
1980s–2000s
Modern globalization
WTO formation, containerization, and the internet supercharged trade. China's rise and supply chain fragmentation transformed global commerce patterns.
2010s–present
New complexities
Trade tensions, digital services trade, and reshoring trends create a more complex landscape — the gravity model remains a key analytical framework.

Summary: The Gravity Model of Trade provides a powerful, intuitive framework for predicting trade volumes. Just as Newton's gravity depends on mass and distance, trade depends on economic size (GDP) and geographic distance. Countries like Canada and Mexico punch above their global weight in US trade due to proximity and agreements — while Germany trades extensively due to sheer economic scale. Understanding these forces helps explain why global trade looks the way it does today.
