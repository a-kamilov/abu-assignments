01 — Foundations

External Economies of Scale

When many firms from the same industry gather in one location, the whole becomes greater than the sum of its parts. Costs fall. Productivity rises. No single firm arranged this — it emerged from proximity itself.

01

Specialized Suppliers

A dense cluster attracts suppliers who specialize in serving that exact industry. Components, raw materials, and services become readily available at lower cost — far cheaper than isolated firms could negotiate alone.

02

Labor Market Pooling

When firms cluster, they collectively train and attract a deep pool of specialized workers. Hiring is faster, skills are sharper, and workers benefit from better mobility and higher wages — a win for everyone.

03

Knowledge Spillovers

Ideas travel fast when engineers, managers, and technicians work near each other. Innovation learned at one firm spills into neighboring firms through conversation, job switching, and shared professional networks.

What Is an Industrial Cluster?

An industrial cluster is a geographic concentration of interconnected companies operating in the same or related industries. The Silicon Valley tech ecosystem, London's financial district, and Qiaotou's button factories are all clusters. Each benefits from a self-reinforcing cycle: more firms attract more suppliers and workers, which attracts more firms.

Crucially, these advantages are external to any single firm. No individual company created them — they arise from the cluster itself. This makes established clusters extraordinarily difficult to displace, even when competing regions offer lower wages.

04 — Deep Dive

Qiaotou, China:
The Button Capital of the World

A small town in Zhejiang Province now supplies approximately 60% of the world's buttons. This is the story of how a cluster forms, grows, and becomes nearly impossible to displace.

From Rural Town to Global Supplier

In the 1980s, a handful of entrepreneurs in Qiaotou began manufacturing simple plastic buttons. What followed was a textbook case of cluster formation: suppliers moved in, workers specialized, knowledge accumulated, and costs plummeted. Today Qiaotou produces over 15 billion buttons annually — not because land or labor is cheapest there, but because the cluster itself generates advantages no new entrant can replicate quickly.

EARLY 1980s

The Seed: First Entrepreneurs

A small group of traders began selling simple buttons sourced from factories. Margins were thin, but demand proved steady. Early success attracted neighbors and family members into the same business, beginning the cluster's first phase of concentration.

LATE 1980s–90s

Supplier Attraction

As the number of button firms grew, specialized raw material suppliers established themselves nearby. Resin suppliers, dyeing services, and mold-makers relocated to Qiaotou, reducing input costs for all cluster members. The first positive feedback loop was underway.

1990s–2000s

Labor Specialization

An entire generation grew up working in button factories, becoming experts in injection molding, quality control, and design. This deep labor pool made hiring fast and training minimal — advantages new competitors could not simply purchase.

2000s–2010s

Knowledge Accumulation & Innovation

Firms began competing on design innovation, not just price. New fastener types, materials, and decorative techniques spread rapidly through the cluster as engineers moved between firms. Qiaotou expanded from simple buttons into zippers, buckles, and related accessories.

TODAY

Unassailable Competitive Advantage

Even though wages in Qiaotou have risen significantly, no competitor has displaced its dominance. The 40 years of accumulated supplier relationships, specialized labor, and embedded knowledge cannot be replicated overnight — a perfect demonstration that the cheapest location is rarely the most competitive one.

05 — Synthesis

What the Cluster Teaches Us

Three dimensions of learning — knowledge gained, skills developed, and insights that change how we see economic geography.

🧠

Knowledge

  • External economies of scale arise from geographic industry concentration, not from a single firm's size
  • Industrial clusters form through three reinforcing mechanisms: specialized suppliers, labor pooling, and knowledge spillovers
  • Cluster advantages are self-reinforcing — once established, they create barriers to geographic competition
  • Historical accident and first-mover effects often determine which location hosts a cluster
⚡

Skills

  • Economic analysis: identifying when location creates competitive advantage
  • Trade theory: understanding how geography shapes international specialization
  • Interpreting why firms choose specific locations beyond simple cost comparisons
  • Evaluating competitive advantages that are structural, not just operational
💡

Reflection

  • The cheapest location is rarely the most competitive — cluster externalities often outweigh wage differentials
  • Businesses gain invisible advantages from nearby suppliers, workers, and knowledge networks that do not appear on any balance sheet
  • Historical advantages compound over decades — first movers can shape an industry's geography for generations
  • Economic geography is not random: it is shaped by path dependence, network effects, and self-reinforcing cycles
