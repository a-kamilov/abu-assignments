The Core Problem

Every business faces limits.

No factory has unlimited workers, materials, or machines. Optimization is about squeezing maximum value from whatever you have.

⏱
Labor Hours

Workers can only be scheduled for so many hours. Each product demands a share of that time.

📦
Raw Materials

Steel, fabric, chemicals — inputs are finite. You can't build more products than the supply allows.

🏭
Machine Capacity

Machines have maximum throughput. Running them beyond capacity causes downtime and defects.

📊
Market Demand

Customers only need so much. Producing beyond demand wastes resources and inflates inventory costs.

Core Concepts

The building blocks of every optimization model.

Linear Programming breaks every business problem into five precise elements. Master these and you can model almost any resource allocation challenge.

01 — Variables

Decision Variables

The quantities you're trying to decide. In production planning, these are typically the number of units to manufacture for each product.

x₁ = units of Product A
x₂ = units of Product B
02 — Constraints

Resource Constraints

Mathematical inequalities that enforce real-world limits — labor hours, material quantities, machine time, and demand ceilings.

2x₁ + 3x₂ ≤ 240 (labor hrs)
x₁ + 2x₂ ≤ 180 (materials)
03 — Objective

Objective Function

The formula that quantifies what you're maximizing — typically profit. Every unit produced adds a known contribution to the total.

Maximize Z = 50x₁ + 75x₂
(profit per unit × quantity)
04 — Region

Feasible Solution

Any production plan that satisfies all constraints simultaneously. Infinitely many feasible plans exist — the solver finds which one is best.

All (x₁, x₂) where every
constraint is satisfied
05 — Answer

Optimal Solution

The single feasible plan that produces the highest possible profit. For linear problems, the optimum always lies at a corner of the feasible region.

The (x₁*, x₂*) that maximizes Z
while respecting all constraints

Real-World Applications

This model runs inside some of the world's largest operations.

🔩
Manufacturing

Automotive and electronics plants use LP to determine daily production schedules across dozens of product lines and shared assembly stations.

🚚
Logistics & Routing

Delivery networks optimize vehicle loads and routing to minimize fuel costs while meeting all time-window requirements.

📦
Supply Chain

Companies allocate constrained inventory across distribution centers to balance service levels against holding costs.

✈️
Transportation

Airlines use optimization to assign aircraft to routes, crew to flights, and gates to departures — simultaneously.

👥
Workforce Planning

Hospitals and call centers build shift schedules that cover demand across every hour while minimizing overtime expenditure.

⚙️
Production Scheduling

Semiconductor fabs sequence thousands of jobs across shared machines to maximize throughput and hit delivery commitments.

Key Takeaways

What every decision-maker should understand.

01

Resources Are Finite

Every business operates within hard limits — and those limits define the boundary of what's possible.

02

Every Decision Has Trade-offs

Producing more of one product almost always means producing less of another. There's no free lunch.

03

Optimization Maximizes Value

Mathematical models explore the entire feasible space and return the provably best answer — not just a good guess.

04

Models Enable Better Decisions

Structuring a problem formally forces clarity about goals, limits, and trade-offs — the real value of the modeling process.
