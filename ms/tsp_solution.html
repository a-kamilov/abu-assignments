01 — Definition

What is the
Traveling Salesman?

Imagine a salesman who must visit a set of cities — each city exactly once — and then return home. The goal is to find the shortest possible route that accomplishes this. Simple to state, extraordinarily difficult to solve.

First studied in the 1930s by mathematicians Karl Menger and Hassler Whitney, TSP has become one of the most intensively studied problems in all of optimization. It belongs to the class of NP-hard problems — meaning no known algorithm can guarantee an optimal solution in polynomial time as the number of cities grows.

With just 10 cities, there are over 180,000 possible routes. With 20 cities: over 60 quadrillion. No computer can brute-force its way through large instances.

Routes = (n − 1)! / 2

For n cities, the number of distinct tours grows factorially. At n = 30, this exceeds the estimated number of atoms in the observable universe.

02 — Mechanics

How Does
It Work?

TSP can be modeled as a graph: cities are nodes, and routes between them are weighted edges. The objective is to find the minimum-weight Hamiltonian cycle — a closed loop that visits every node exactly once.

1

Define Cities

Represent each location as a point in space with coordinates. Distance is typically Euclidean, but can account for real-world travel times.

2

Build Distance Matrix

Calculate the cost (distance, time, or money) between every pair of cities — forming an n×n matrix of all possible edge weights.

3

Search for Tour

Apply an algorithm — exact or approximate — to explore the space of possible routes and find one that minimizes total distance.

4

Evaluate & Improve

Iteratively test modifications — swapping city order, reversing sub-tours — and keep improvements until no further gains can be found.

03 — Solution Methods

Approaches to
Solving TSP

No single method dominates for all cases. The choice of algorithm depends on the number of cities, required accuracy, and available computation time.

Brute Force
O((n−1)!)

Enumerate every possible permutation of cities and select the shortest. Guaranteed optimal, but completely impractical beyond roughly 12 cities due to factorial growth.

Exact
Nearest Neighbor
O(n²)

A greedy heuristic: always travel to the closest unvisited city. Fast and simple, but typically yields routes 20–25% longer than optimal. Good as a starting point.

Heuristic
Dynamic Programming
O(n² · 2ⁿ)

Held-Karp algorithm stores results of sub-problems to avoid recomputation. Optimal and feasible up to ~20–25 cities; still exponential, but far better than brute force.

Exact
2-Opt / 3-Opt
O(n²) per pass

Local search improvement: repeatedly reverse segments of the tour if doing so reduces total length. Highly effective in practice and easy to implement alongside other methods.

Local Search
Genetic Algorithm
Configurable

Inspired by natural selection: maintain a population of tours, select the fittest, cross them over, and mutate offspring. Scales to thousands of cities with near-optimal results.

Metaheuristic
Christofides
≤ 1.5× optimal

A polynomial-time approximation with a proven guarantee: the tour is at most 1.5× the optimal. Uses minimum spanning trees and minimum weight matching — a landmark result from 1976.

04 — Real-World Impact

Why Does
It Matter?

TSP is not merely academic. Variants of this problem govern billions of decisions made every day in logistics, manufacturing, and infrastructure.

📦

Last-Mile Delivery

UPS, FedEx, and Amazon optimize thousands of delivery routes daily — shaving seconds per stop translates to millions in fuel savings annually.

🔬

DNA Sequencing

Sequencing genome fragments requires ordering overlapping DNA strands — mathematically equivalent to finding a short Hamiltonian path.

🖨️

PCB Drilling

Manufacturing circuit boards requires drilling thousands of holes; minimizing drill-head travel is a direct TSP application reducing production time.

✈️

Flight Scheduling

Airlines optimize crew rotations, gate assignments, and aircraft routing using TSP-based models to reduce costs and turnaround times.

🤖

Robotics

Warehouse robots, surgical systems, and autonomous vehicles all employ TSP-style optimization to plan efficient movement through constrained spaces.

🌐

Network Routing

Optimizing data packet transmission, laying fibre optic cables, and planning infrastructure all rely on related shortest-path problems.
