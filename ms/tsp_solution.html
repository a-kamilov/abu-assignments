01 — Activity Overview

About This
Project

This project was completed as part of a Management Science course activity focused on optimization and decision-making under constraints. The objective was to investigate the Traveling Salesman Problem — a classic combinatorial optimization challenge — and understand why finding the shortest route becomes increasingly difficult as the number of locations grows.

Project Objectives

Through this activity, the goal was to understand and demonstrate each of the following outcomes:

  • 01Define the Traveling Salesman Problem and explain what makes it computationally challenging as a routing problem.
  • 02Explore how the number of possible routes grows factorially with each additional city, making brute-force search impractical at scale.
  • 03Apply and compare optimization approaches — including nearest neighbor heuristics and 2-opt improvement — to find efficient solutions.
  • 04Connect TSP concepts to real-world Management Science applications in logistics, delivery routing, and resource allocation.
  • 05Reflect on how optimization algorithms support better decision-making in situations where evaluating every option is infeasible.

02 — Problem Statement

Defining the
Problem

The Traveling Salesman Problem poses a deceptively simple question: given a set of cities and the distances between them, what is the shortest route a traveler can take to visit every city exactly once and return to where they started?

Despite how straightforward it sounds, this problem belongs to a class of problems that have no known efficient general solution. It is formally classified as NP-hard — meaning that as the number of cities grows, the time needed to guarantee an optimal answer grows faster than any polynomial function.

📍

Visit Every City Exactly Once

The traveler must pass through each location in the set — no skipping, no revisiting. Every node in the graph must appear in the tour exactly one time.

🔄

Return to the Starting Point

The route must form a complete loop — a closed Hamiltonian cycle — ending at the same city where it began, making it a circular rather than a one-way journey.

📏

Minimize Total Distance

Among all valid tours, the objective is to find the one with the smallest total travel distance (or cost), requiring comparison across a rapidly expanding set of possibilities.

Possible Tours = (n − 1)! ÷ 2

For n cities, the number of distinct tours grows factorially. With just 10 cities there are 181,440 routes. With 20 cities: over 60 quadrillion. No computer can exhaustively check large instances.

Live animation — tour exploration across 8 nodes

Graph Representation

Cities are modeled as nodes. Paths between them are weighted edges. The goal is to find the minimum-weight Hamiltonian cycle through all nodes.

Distance Matrix

All pairwise distances are stored in an n×n matrix. The algorithm references this matrix when evaluating and comparing candidate routes.

Factorial Growth

Each new city multiplies the number of possible routes by (n−1), causing exponential-like growth that outpaces any linear improvement in computing power.

2

Heuristics Are Essential

For real problems with dozens or hundreds of locations, we must accept approximate solutions — good enough routes found quickly — rather than provably optimal ones.

3

P ≠ NP (Probably)

It is widely believed (but not proven) that no polynomial-time algorithm exists for TSP. This makes it one of the great open questions in computer science.

4

Local Search Works

Methods like 2-opt — which swaps route segments to reduce distance — reliably find near-optimal solutions in practical time by improving iteratively rather than searching exhaustively.

04 — Method & Demonstration

Approach &
Solver

For this activity, two methods were applied in sequence to find efficient tours: a Nearest Neighbor greedy construction heuristic to build an initial route, followed by 2-Opt local search to iteratively improve it by eliminating crossing edges.

This combination reflects a practical Management Science approach: start with a fast, reasonable solution, then refine it systematically until no further improvements can be found within the chosen method's scope.

01

Represent Cities as Nodes

Each location is placed on a coordinate plane. Distances between all pairs are computed and stored for reference during optimization.

02

Build Initial Tour (Nearest Neighbor)

Starting from city 1, always travel next to the closest unvisited city. Repeat until all cities are visited, then return to start. This gives a reasonable — though not optimal — starting route.

03

Improve with 2-Opt Swaps

Examine every pair of edges in the tour. If reversing the segment between them shortens the total distance, apply the swap. Repeat until no improvement is found.

04

Compare Before & After

Record the initial tour length and the optimized tour length. The percentage reduction demonstrates the value of iterative improvement over a greedy starting point.

Why This Approach?

Nearest Neighbor is a well-known construction heuristic that produces tours roughly 20–25% above optimal on average. It is fast and easy to understand — making it an ideal starting point for a course activity.

2-Opt then corrects the most obvious inefficiencies by eliminating route crossings, which are always suboptimal in Euclidean space. Together, they demonstrate the value of combining a fast heuristic with a structured improvement phase.

05 — Findings & Observations

What the
Results Showed

Across the optimization activity, several clear patterns emerged. The following findings summarize the key observations from applying nearest neighbor construction and 2-opt improvement to the TSP.

F1
Finding 01

Optimization Reduces Distance Significantly

The 2-opt improvement phase consistently produced shorter routes than the initial greedy construction — often reducing total tour length by 15–25%, demonstrating that the starting route is rarely the best achievable.

F2
Finding 02

Greedy Is Fast but Leaves Gaps

Nearest Neighbor produces a visually sensible route very quickly, but frequently creates suboptimal crossings that a simple improvement step can fix. The gap between greedy and optimal is real and measurable.

F3
Finding 03

Small Problems Are Manageable

With fewer than 12 cities, multiple solution approaches — including brute force — become feasible. The activity highlighted that problem size is the primary constraint on which methods are practical.

F4
Finding 04

Complexity Grows Faster Than Expected

The route count table made clear how non-intuitive factorial growth is. Adding 5 cities to a 10-city problem multiplied the possible routes by a factor of over 200,000 — a visceral demonstration of combinatorial explosion.

F5
Finding 05

Route Efficiency Has Real Cost Implications

Every unit of unnecessary distance in a route translates to real cost in logistics scenarios. Even a 10% improvement in route length, applied across hundreds of daily deliveries, compounds into substantial operational savings.

F6
Finding 06

Local Optima Are Not Global Optima

2-Opt terminates when no single swap improves the tour — but this locally optimal solution is not necessarily the globally shortest route. This demonstrates a key limitation of local search methods.

06 — What I Learned

Student
Reflection

Before this activity, optimization felt abstract — a concept from textbooks rather than something with tangible, practical stakes. Working through the Traveling Salesman Problem changed that. When I generated a set of cities and watched the solver run, the gap between a naively constructed route and the 2-opt improved one was immediately visible. That visual difference is real cost — real fuel, real time, real money.

What struck me most was the complexity table. I understood intellectually that factorial growth was fast, but seeing the number of possible routes for 20 cities written out — sixty quadrillion — made it concrete in a way that an equation alone never had. No amount of computing power solves that by checking every option. The only path forward is smarter, not faster.

This is what Management Science is really about: recognizing when a problem is too large to solve perfectly, and designing a method that finds a solution good enough to act on — reliably, quickly, and with understood trade-offs. TSP is a model for that broader mindset.

🧠

Combinatorial Optimization

Problems with discrete choices and factorial solution spaces require fundamentally different approaches than continuous optimization.

🔍

Heuristics vs. Exact Methods

Heuristics sacrifice guaranteed optimality for practical speed — a trade-off that is often not just acceptable but necessary in real applications.

📈

Complexity Scales Unexpectedly

Factorial growth is deeply non-intuitive. Even small problem sizes rapidly reach the limits of what computation can guarantee.

🏭

Real-World Relevance

TSP directly models logistics, delivery routing, manufacturing, and network planning — problems every organization with physical operations faces.

♻️

Iterative Improvement

Starting with a feasible solution and refining it is often more effective than attempting to construct perfection from scratch.

07 — Course Connection

Management Science
Relevance

The Traveling Salesman Problem is not just a mathematical curiosity — it is a direct embodiment of the core themes studied in Management Science. This project sits squarely within the discipline's concern for systematic, quantitative approaches to complex real-world decisions.

⚖️

Decision Making

TSP forces structured thinking about choices: which city to visit next, which route to evaluate, when to accept a solution. These are decision frameworks applicable far beyond routing.

🎯

Optimization

The core objective — minimize total distance — mirrors every optimization problem in operations: minimize cost, maximize throughput, reduce waste. TSP is a prototype of the form.

💡

Resource Efficiency

Shorter routes mean lower fuel consumption, fewer labor hours, and reduced vehicle wear. Optimization directly translates to measurable resource savings at scale.

🌐

Real-World Application

Last-mile delivery, airline routing, PCB manufacturing, warehouse robotics — TSP variants power decisions made billions of times daily across global industries.

📐

Quantitative Methods

TSP demonstrates how mathematical modeling — graph theory, distance matrices, algorithmic analysis — enables rigorous, reproducible analysis of complex operational problems.

🔄

Iterative Reasoning

Management decisions rarely arrive at perfect solutions immediately. TSP illustrates how structured iteration — building, evaluating, improving — leads to better outcomes than intuition alone.
